### Pillar 1: Doppelgängers & Identity in XR
 
- People are bad at noticing when a VR identity has been swapped or replaced by an AI copy — a real experiment found ~40% of an audience missed a live avatar swap.
- Researchers have also started building prototypes where an AI "stand-in" represents someone who isn't actually there (e.g. an AI double attending a meeting on your behalf).
- **But:** nobody has built a proper, controlled way to actually test this kind of system. What exists is just a one-off demo and an early prototype — not an evaluated research platform.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** by evaluating whether the SDK can serve as that missing platform.
### Pillar 2: How Virtual Agents Are Controlled
 
- There are three known ways to control a virtual agent: a human secretly operates it (Wizard-of-Oz), a human writes rules to constrain an AI, or the AI runs fully on its own.
- All three have been studied — and each has known problems (WoZ tires out the human operator; fully autonomous AI is unpredictable).
- **But:** no one has tested all three approaches on the *same* tool, side by side.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** — that's exactly what the three test conditions (A/B/C) in RQ2 do.
### Pillar 3: How to Judge a Research Toolkit
 
- There's an accepted method for judging whether a research toolkit is well-designed (Ledo et al.'s four-part framework).
- The SDK's own creators already used a version of this method to check their own tool.
- **But:** they admit it themselves — their test was small (4 developers), informal, and mostly involved experts, not real newcomers.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** by running a proper, independent version of that check (RQ1's checklist and RQ3's usage study).
