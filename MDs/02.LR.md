
## 3. Literature Review — Brief Summary

**Pillar 1 (Doppelgängers & identity in XR)** shows that identity substitution in
shared VR is both perceptually unstable and increasingly used as a deliberate design
feature (e.g. Oliva et al.'s 2026 avatar-swap study; Senel et al.'s change-blindness
findings; Bai et al.'s SEAM stand-in system). This establishes *why* doppelgänger-style
agents are a legitimate and timely research object — motivating RQ1 and RQ2.

**Pillar 2 (Control paradigms for virtual agents)** reviews the spectrum from
Wizard-of-Oz (fully human-controlled) through constrained-AI to fully autonomous
LLM-driven agents, drawing on WOz tooling literature (Bellucci et al.) and recent
embodied-agent WOz studies, alongside the SDK's own LLM-driven architecture. This
supplies the rationale for RQ2's three demonstration conditions across the autonomy
spectrum.

**Pillar 3 (HCI toolkit evaluation)** establishes the methodological basis for the
whole dissertation: Ledo et al.'s (2018) four evaluation strategies, two of which —
demonstration and usage — are already reflected in the SDK's own authors' evaluation
practice (Li et al., 2026), giving this project both a methodologically-grounded
framework (RQ1, RQ3) and a prior baseline to compare against.

**The gap this dissertation addresses**: doppelgänger-style AI agents are increasingly
studied as a phenomenon, and toolkit evaluation methodology exists, but no independent
study has yet applied a structured, Ledo-style evaluation specifically to assess
whether a current-generation LLM-driven IVA SDK is fit for controlled doppelgänger
research across the human-control-to-autonomous spectrum. This is the gap RQ0 (and its sub-questions RQ1–RQ3) fills.


### Pillar 1: Doppelgängers & Identity in XR
 
- People are bad at noticing when a VR identity has been swapped or replaced by an AI copy — a real experiment found ~40% of an audience missed a live avatar swap.
- Researchers have also started building prototypes where an AI "stand-in" represents someone who isn't actually there (e.g. an AI double attending a meeting on your behalf).
- **But:** nobody has built a proper, controlled way to actually test this kind of system. What exists is just a one-off demo and an early prototype — not an evaluated research platform.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** by evaluating whether the SDK can serve as that missing platform.
### Pillar 2: How Virtual Agents Are Controlled
 
- There are three known ways to control a virtual agent: a human secretly operates it (Wizard-of-Oz), a human writes rules to constrain an AI, or the AI runs fully on its own.
- All three have been studied — and each has known problems (WoZ tires out the human operator; fully autonomous AI is unpredictable).
- **But:** no one has tested all three approaches on the *same* tool, side by side.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** — that's exactly what the three test conditions (A/B/C) in RQ2 do.
### Pillar 3: How to Judge a Research Toolkit
 
- There's an accepted method for judging whether a research toolkit is well-designed (Ledo et al.'s four-part framework).
- The SDK's own creators already used a version of this method to check their own tool.
- **But:** they admit it themselves — their test was small (4 developers), informal, and mostly involved experts, not real newcomers.
- **This dissertation fills that gap** by running a proper, independent version of that check (RQ1's checklist and RQ3's usage study).

**What this table makes visible (the "gap" argument in one line):** the doppelgänger/identity literature (row 5) documents real perceptual and ethical risk but no controlled experimental platform; the WoZ literature (rows 6–9) offers mature methodology for the human-controlled end of the spectrum but stops short of evaluating LLM-driven autonomy; and the SDK's own paper (row 4) explicitly flags its own evaluation as small-sample and exploratory. This dissertation's three-condition RQ2 design and independent RQ1/RQ3 evaluation sit directly in that combined gap.
